# Do authentic data revisions change the decision? A real-time vintage study, corrected

**Draft research paper, version 3.** Branch `research/real-world-revision-study`. Version 1 of this
study was invalid for the model the protocol defines: the audit-time objective was constructed with the
wrong coefficient mapping (`z_A - 2 z_D` instead of `-z_A`). Version 2 repaired the design. Version 3 is
a study-only comparison pass: it adds the KKT + feasible-descent baseline the review asked for, separates
CRI-U's three uncertainty questions, makes the storage comparison functionally equivalent and fixes the
reanalysis labelling. It changes no platform code and no part of CRI's mathematical core. The correction
log — with every defect, its detection, its repair, its verification and its numerical impact — is
[CORRECTIONS.md](CORRECTIONS.md); the superseded results are preserved unchanged under
`artifacts/research/rtds/superseded/` (`results_v1.json`, `results_v2.json`).

**Sample label.** The preregistered 2015–2024H1 test split is reported as a **post-results reanalysis**,
not as an untouched confirmation; version 1's inspection of it preceded these corrections. The only
untouched sample is 2024H2–2025H1, reported descriptively in §4.7.

Every number below is generated by the scripts in `scripts/research/rtds_*.py` and rendered into
`artifacts/research/rtds/tables.md`. The protocol and its amendment record are
[PREREGISTRATION.md](PREREGISTRATION.md).

## Abstract

Published macroeconomic observations are revised, and much is known about how large those revisions
are. Much less is known about whether they change *decisions*. We assemble 2,347 real-time vintage
responses (675 distinct release dates) over seven US series from ALFRED, forming 408 decision/audit
pairs spanning 2000–2025 in two separate revision processes, and run one fixed convex program on them,
measuring decision regret by re-solving with two independent solvers. Every audit-time program is
additionally built directly from the audit-vintage observations and asserted equal to the one produced
by the revision compiler (maximum disagreement 8.9e-16 over 408 events). In the **post-results
reanalysis** of the preregistered 2015–2024H1 split, **73.7%** (95% clustered interval 41.7–91.7%) of
quarterly BEA expenditure revisions and **50.0%** (30.6–63.9%) of monthly BLS payroll revisions change
the optimal decision by more than 1e-3 objective units. Four negative results now dominate the study.
First, the two-sided workflow — a certificate for the immaterial direction, a feasible descent for the
material one — reaches 94.7% coverage, but **the certificate contributes nothing to it**: a combined
KKT-stability + greedy-descent baseline publishes *identical* verdicts on all 152 events (0
disagreements, identical certified sets, 0 immaterialities and 0 materialities added by CRI), slightly
cheaper. Second, plain OSQP re-solving is faster than either combined workflow (0.93–0.97 ms per audit
against 1.30–2.08 ms) and settles every event with no abstentions, so at these dimensions the simplest
workflow does not merely match certification — it dominates it. Third, CRI-U certifies nothing under
any of its three questions, including a post-audit set centred on the observed revision, and the
attempted guarantee fails at the tolerance rather than at the model. Fourth, the stored-state advantage
claimed in v1 does not exist once both workflows are made functionally equivalent: the certificate
record is 1.20× the re-solve record, not a fraction of it. The genuine findings that remain are
descriptive: authentic revisions move the decision often; revision magnitude is uninformative about
whether they do; the certificate is sound but redundant with the active-set test; and the large
geometry AUC of v1 was carried by a directional diagnostic that costs ≈37 ms and 28–37 KKT checks per
event.

## 1. Question, and what is not claimed

*Of the authentic revisions examined, how many materially change the decision, and what can be
established without re-solving?*

The economic observations are real vintage data. **The decisions are not historical.** They are produced
by a convex program this study defines, so this is a counterfactual decision-model study, not an audit of
decisions anyone took. The growth-to-cost mapping is declared, not estimated from outcomes. Nothing here
is a new theorem: the certificate is weak duality plus an active-set argument, applied to real revisions.

## 2. Data and audit horizon

Source: **ALFRED**, the archival companion of FRED, read through `alfredgraph.csv?id=…&vintage_date=…`,
which returns the vintage series rather than the current one (verified before the protocol was written:
GDPC1 for 2014-10-01 reads 16,311.6 in the 2015-01-30 vintage, 16,294.7 in 2015-03-27 and 16,186.7 in
2016-07-29).

| cohort | frequency | series | vintage responses | train | pilot | test | holdout |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | quarterly | `GDPC1`, `GPDIC1`, `GCEC1`, `EXPGSC1` | 1,338 | 52 | 8 | 38 | 4 |
| B | monthly | `PAYEMS`, `MANEMP`, `USCONS` | 1,009 | 156 | 24 | 114 | 12 |

**Horizon (repaired).** The protocol specifies four calendar quarters (A) and twelve calendar months
(B) — the same calendar interval. The audit vintage is the first release on or after
`add_months(decision, 12)`, with month-end clipping. The earlier implementation used 400 and 366 days;
400 days is about thirteen months, which moved almost every quarterly audit vintage one release later.
98 of 98 paired quarterly events changed audit vintage; because an adjacent release usually carries the
same value for an old period, only 3 of them changed the standardised growth vector.

**Time policy.** A vintage date is a release date. It is treated as available at 23:59:59 UTC on that
date, and no intraday timestamp is claimed anywhere.

**Revision categories are not pooled.** Advance/second/later estimates and the annual-benchmark window
are labelled from the release calendar, and the two cohorts are analysed separately: pooling a quarterly
expenditure revision with a monthly payroll revision would be a statistic about the average of two
unrelated things. No revision is described as an error; a revision is a new estimate, not a correction of
a mistake.

**Accessibility findings.** The Philadelphia Fed's Real-Time Data Set for Macroeconomists was assessed
first and not used: its documented data-file paths now return the site's 404 page. `PCEC1` has no ALFRED
vintage archive (its download page 404s), so the protocol's personal-consumption series could not be
built; the cohort uses `EXPGSC1` (real exports) in its place — the fourth real expenditure aggregate with
a complete archive. That substitution is a data-availability convenience, not an economic argument, and
it is recorded as amendment A0 rather than folded silently into the design.
ALFRED's edge stalls on any non-default `curl` User-Agent and intermittently drops HTTP/2 streams; the
fetch uses the default agent, HTTP/1.1 and bounded retries.

**Rights.** ALFRED/FRED data are redistributed by the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis under its terms
of use; the underlying series are US government statistics in the public domain. This repository stores
derived event records, URLs and SHA-256 digests, not the vendor's raw files.

## 3. Design

**The convention, stated once.** The program is `min 0.5 x'Hx + c'x` with `H = rho·corr(z)` fixed across
vintages and `c = -z`. A revision is a change in the **linear cost vector**, applied with a positive
identity sensitivity: `delta = c_A - c_D = z_D - z_A`, so the compiled audit program satisfies
`c_D + delta == -z_A`. The two possible conventions (change in expected returns with a negative
sensitivity, or change in costs with a positive one) are never mixed.

**Runtime equivalence.** For every event the audit program is also built directly from the audit-vintage
observations, with no revision machinery involved, and asserted equal in objective coefficients, quadratic
matrix, bounds, budget, inequality rows and right-hand sides, and objective and gradient at the recorded
decision and four probe points. This is the guard against the v1 defect and is a permanent test.

**Program** (identical structure in both cohorts): `Σx = 1`, `0 ≤ x ≤ 0.6`, plus `x_gov ≥ 0.15`,
`x_inv ≤ 0.45` (A) or `x_manuf ≥ 0.20` (B). `c` is train-split standardised growth; `ρ = 1.0` by a
prespecified train-only rule that proved non-discriminating (see the amendment record), defended instead
by the sensitivity grid in §4.8.

**Three-way classification.** An upper bound below the tolerance proves immateriality. An upper bound
above it proves nothing, so materiality needs a lower bound, which comes from a feasible descent: a
budget-preserving swap search (closed-form steps, no solver) whose point satisfies `f(x_repair) ≥ f*`,
hence `f(x_D) - f(x_repair) ≤ regret`. Events with neither claim are reported as unknown.

**Statistics.** Moving-block bootstrap over decision years (the clustering unit), 2,000 resamples;
cluster bootstrap for AUC; one primary test per cohort, everything else secondary.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Revisions do change decisions, and more often than v1 reported

| cohort | test events | material at τ=1e-3 | 95% cluster interval | v1 (invalid model) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 38 | 24 → **28 (73.7%)** | [41.7%, 91.7%] | 63.2% |
| B | 114 | 54 → **57 (50.0%)** | [30.6%, 63.9%] | 47.4% |

Attribution (same events): with the corrected convention but the *old* audit vintages, A reads 27/38 and
B 57/114. So the sign error, not the horizon repair, accounts for most of the difference — and in cohort
B for all of it. Both lower bounds exceed the protocol's 10% threshold, so H1 holds in both cohorts.

**This split is a reanalysis, not a confirmation.** 2015–2024H1 had already been inspected when the
corrections of §3 were made; the numbers above are what the corrected design says about the same period,
not a fresh test of it. The only untouched sample is 2024H2–2025H1 (§4.7), and it is too small in
cohort A to confirm anything. Every table in this paper therefore carries the label *post-results
reanalysis*.

### 4.2 Certification: coverage, abstention, and no false certificates

| cohort | certified immaterial | material witness | unknown | decisions certified | false immaterial | false material | immaterial events covered |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 8 (21.1%) [8.3%, 50.0%] | 28 (73.7%) | 2 (5.3%) | 94.7% | 0 | 0 | 8/10 |
| B | 51 (44.7%) [36.1%, 63.9%] | 57 (50.0%) | 6 (5.3%) | 94.7% | 0 | 0 | 51/57 |

Every test event has a nonzero revision, so these are genuine nonzero numerical changes whose decision
impact is provably below (or provably above) the threshold. The certificate never certifies a material
revision as immaterial, and the witness never fires on an immaterial one — the latter is structural, since
a lower bound cannot exceed the true regret, and it is asserted per event.

Note what the certified share does *not* mean: it is not accuracy, and v1's 92.1%/89.5% "accuracy"
figures are withdrawn (§C3 of the correction log). An upper bound above τ is a failure to prove, not a
proof of the opposite.

### 4.3 The simpler baseline reproduces the whole result, and OSQP dominates both

The reference in the review was right, and it changes the headline: the 94.7% coverage is a property of
a **two-sided workflow**, not of CRI. The material half is produced by a greedy feasible descent — a
partial reoptimisation, not a free step — and the immaterial half coincides with ordinary KKT stability.
The head-to-head comparison makes this explicit, with each workflow's own verdict rule and its own
runtime.

| cohort | workflow | immaterial | material | abstentions | settled | errors | audit (ms) | +compile (ms) | end to end (ms) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | KKT only | 8 | 0 | 30 | 21.1% | 0 | 1.16 | 1.38 | 5.36 |
| A | KKT + greedy descent | 8 | 28 | 2 | 94.7% | 0 | 1.37 | 1.61 | 5.77 |
| A | CRI only | 8 | 0 | 30 | 21.1% | 0 | 1.83 | 2.05 | 6.02 |
| A | CRI + greedy descent | 8 | 28 | 2 | 94.7% | 0 | 2.08 | 2.29 | 6.28 |
| A | OSQP re-solve | 10 | 28 | 0 | 100.0% | 0 | **0.97** | 1.22 | **5.29** |
| B | KKT only | 51 | 0 | 63 | 44.7% | 0 | 1.18 | 1.40 | 5.35 |
| B | KKT + greedy descent | 51 | 57 | 6 | 94.7% | 0 | 1.30 | 1.52 | 5.47 |
| B | CRI only | 51 | 0 | 63 | 44.7% | 0 | 1.80 | 2.01 | 5.95 |
| B | CRI + greedy descent | 51 | 57 | 6 | 94.7% | 0 | 1.93 | 2.16 | 6.05 |
| B | OSQP re-solve | 57 | 57 | 0 | 100.0% | 0 | **0.93** | 1.16 | **5.18** |

| cohort | events | verdict disagreements | certified sets identical | added by CRI (immaterial) | added by CRI (material) | KKT-stable but bound > τ | CRI immaterial without KKT stability |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 38 | 0 | yes | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| B | 114 | 0 | yes | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |

The two combined workflows never disagree, in either cohort, on any event: the certificate and the
active-set test certify exactly the same set, and both materiality sides are the same descent. So on
this data the certificate's **incremental classification utility over KKT + greedy descent is zero**,
and its incremental cost is real (0.6–0.7 ms per audit over the KKT test, plus the record size of §4.6).

The re-solve baseline goes further. OSQP answers the question directly, has no abstentions (it computes
the regret), makes no errors, and is the fastest of the five at 0.93–0.97 ms per audit — 30% cheaper
than KKT + descent and 2.1× cheaper than CRI + descent, and still 5–10% cheaper end to end once
compilation and the original solve are charged to all of them. What the certificate buys is not speed
or accuracy but a different kind of object: a *validated inequality* rather than a computed number,
with the multipliers as its witness. That property is what makes the certificate redundant here — in a
convex program, a valid bound below τ and KKT stability at the recorded point are the same fact — and
it is why the redundancy survived a change of convention, of horizons and of data.

We therefore state the comparison plainly: **for audit workflows at this scale, CRI is dominated by
both KKT + greedy descent and OSQP**. It is not wrong, and its bound is the only one of the objects
that remains valid when the decision moves — but the bound is loose exactly where that matters (median
bound/regret 4.26 and 5.26 on material events, median bound 0.135 and 0.108 against τ = 1e-3).

The claim "magnitude is uninformative" survives, and is stronger than in v1 (0.425/0.540, i.e. at or
below chance; v1 said 0.49/0.61). The claim "geometry matters" survives only in a weaker form: without
the directional stability radius, the AUC is 0.675 [CI includes 0.5] in cohort A and 0.737 in cohort B.
The v1 headline of 0.88/0.94 came from a feature that repeatedly re-runs the KKT test along the observed
revision direction — a target-proximal diagnostic, not free metadata. Charging its cost makes the point
concrete: 41 ms and 28–37 KKT checks per event, against 1.8 ms to certify and 0.93–0.97 ms for an OSQP
re-solve. Using it as a triage rule would be strictly worse than re-solving, so it is reported as a
diagnostic and not as a discovery.

The metadata group is also structurally limited here: at a twelve-month horizon every input is revised in
every event, so "number of changing inputs" has no variance.

### 4.5 CRI-U: three different questions, three zeros, and only one of them is about the observed revision

The earlier version reported two zero-certification numbers and read them as a property of CRI-U on
historical revisions. The two sets were both centred on **no change**, which answers a prospective
question: *if the correction were any vector in a box of typical size around zero, would the decision
still be certified immaterial?* That is not the same as asking about the correction that occurred. The
three questions are now asked separately, with widths from the training split only.

| cohort | arm | base | centre | certificates | contained | certified given contained | median bound |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | prospective, audit base | audit program | zero (further change) | 0/38 | not measurable | n/a | 1.476 |
| A | prospective, decision base | decision program | zero (no change) | 0/38 | 19/38 (50.0%) | 0.0% | 0.860 |
| A | post-audit, observed centre | decision program | observed revision | 0/38 | 38/38 (by construction) | 0.0% | 1.910 |
| B | prospective, audit base | audit program | zero (further change) | 0/114 | not measurable | n/a | 1.065 |
| B | prospective, decision base | decision program | zero (no change) | 0/114 | 56/114 (49.1%) | 0.0% | 0.580 |
| B | post-audit, observed centre | decision program | observed revision | 0/114 | 114/114 (by construction) | 0.0% | 1.579 |

Three things follow, and they are different claims.

* **The prospective guarantee is conditional, and the condition fails half the time.** The observed
  correction lies inside the zero-centred decision-base box for 19 of 38 and 56 of 114 events, so even a
  certificate there would speak only to those events; it certifies none of them either.
* **Centring on the observed revision does not rescue it.** With the set now built around the actual
  `Δc = c_A − c_D`, the guarantee applies to the historical revision by construction (containment
  100%), and the bound is still 0.58–1.91, i.e. 580–1,910 times τ. The failure is the tolerance against
the size of revisions, not the choice of centre.
* **The audit-base arm is not testable with two vintages.** It asks about a correction that has not
  arrived; no containment rate is quoted for it, and none should be.

So the earlier 0/38 and 0/114 were neither a general failure of CRI-U (the sets did not contain the
observed revisions half the time) nor a confirmed one (the arm about the observed revision had not been
run). What the corrected comparison supports is narrower and still negative: at τ = 1e-3, a worst-case
certificate over a box sized by typical revisions cannot certify an immaterial decision, under any of the
three questions. Verification of the machinery (bound sound against exhaustive vertex enumeration,
singleton-at-zero reproducing CRI, level-as-centre inflating the bound, observed-centred set containing
the observed revision) is in `tests/test_rtds_design.py`.

### 4.6 Cost and stored state

| cohort | certificate | active-set | compile | repair | internal re-solve | warm re-solve | OSQP re-solve | radius search |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 1.83 ms | 1.16 ms | 0.23 ms | 0.18 ms | 3.68 ms | 3.89 ms | **0.97 ms** | 37.29 ms |
| B | 1.80 ms | 1.18 ms | 0.23 ms | 0.11 ms | 4.11 ms | 4.14 ms | **0.93 ms** | 37.18 ms |

Certification beats the project's own SLSQP re-solve (1.8 vs 3.7–4.1 ms) and its warm start, and loses to
OSQP by about 2×. Combined with §4.3 that is the honest summary: at this scale, re-solving is cheapest,
KKT + descent is next, and CRI + descent is last while producing the same verdicts. Timings move with
the machine; the ordering and the ratios are the claim.

**Stored state (bytes per decision), functionally equivalent arms.**

| cohort | decision state + provenance | CRI record (with revision) | re-solve record | shared block (once) | amortized shared | CRI arm | re-solve arm | ratio | full problem |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 704 | 970 | 804 | 916 | 9.0 | 978.0 | 813.0 | 1.20 | 1,080 |
| B | 661 | 883 | 741 | 666 | 2.2 | 885.2 | 743.2 | 1.19 | 807 |

The earlier comparison charged the recorded decision to only one arm: it set a 736–673 byte certificate
record against an 83–63 byte cost vector, and a re-solve cannot audit anything from a cost vector without
the recorded weights and the vintage metadata. Both arms now carry identical per-decision state — weights,
decision-time costs, clocks, vintages, horizon, digests, observation provenance — and the same shared
block, including cohort provenance and the manifest digest. The only difference is the auditor's extra:
multipliers plus the revision delta for CRI, the audit-time cost vector for re-solving. The comparison is
therefore like for like, and **CRI's record is 1.19–1.20× the re-solve record, not a fraction of it**. The
claimed storage advantage does not exist at any framing we could defend.

What the record does offer is different and narrower: a fresh process reconstructs and verifies the
certificate from the record, the shared block and the newly available revision data alone — demonstrated
for 376 records with a worst bound gap of 3.6e-15 (`scripts/research/rtds_audit_record.py --verify`).

Materiality and certifiability both depend on the risk weight: with a nearly linear objective the
decision sits on a corner, revisions rarely move it, and certification rises to 66–68%. The qualitative
conclusions hold across the grid; the percentages do not.

Two sensitivity families the protocol specified were **not executed**: the mapping variants (raw growth,
log growth, sign only) and the horizon grid (1/2/4 quarters; 3/6/12 months). Both require re-deriving the
whole pipeline rather than re-thresholding it. They are reported here as **limitations, not as evidence**:
nothing in this paper is a test of a different functional form or a different horizon, and no statement
about the generality of the materiality rate, the redundancy result or the geometry AUC across mappings
and horizons is supported. Every claim above is conditional on the declared mapping `c = -z` and on a
twelve-calendar-month audit horizon. The record is amendment A10; the same limitation is repeated in §6
so that a reader who skips this subsection still sees it.

## 5. What survived, what did not

**Survived (as description, on a reanalysed sample)**

1. Revisions change decisions often: 50.0% and 73.7% of the 2015–2024H1 reanalysis events, with both
   cluster intervals excluding 10%.
2. Revision magnitude is uninformative about materiality, at or below chance.
3. The certificate is sound — zero false immateriality certificates, in both cohorts, under both versions
   — and covers 80% and 89.5% of the immaterial events.
4. The certified set is exactly the classical active-set set; the redundancy survived a change of
   convention, of horizons and of data.
5. CRI-U certifies nothing at τ = 1e-3 under any of its three questions, and the reason is now quantified
   (bounds 580–1,910× τ) rather than attributed to a bug.

**Did not survive**

6. **CRI's incremental utility over the simpler workflow — withdrawn, and this is now the central
   result.** KKT stability + greedy descent reproduces every verdict, at slightly lower cost; OSQP
   re-solving reproduces every verdict with no abstentions and is faster than both. The 94.7% coverage is
   a property of a two-sided workflow whose material half is partial reoptimisation.
7. "Geometry predicts materiality with AUC 0.88/0.94" — **weakened**. Without the directional radius it is
   0.675/0.737, and with it the method costs ≈37 ms per event against 0.93–0.97 ms to re-solve.
8. "The audit record is 366–429 bytes, a large storage saving" — **withdrawn**. On functionally
   equivalent arms the certificate record is 1.19–1.20× the re-solve record, and larger in absolute terms.
9. "CRI accuracy 92.1%/89.5%" — **withdrawn**. It counted uninformative upper bounds as materiality
   claims.
10. All v1 materiality, certification and AUC figures computed on `z_A - 2 z_D` — **invalid**, preserved
    as superseded.

### The central negative result, and the question worth asking next

The study's most defensible empirical statement is now a negative one: **on real revisions, at these
dimensions, with an OSQP re-solve available, CRI provides no measurable incremental utility** — not in
coverage (identical to KKT + descent), not in errors (both zero), not in speed (slower than both), and not
in storage (1.2× larger). The two-sided workflow does work, and the 94.7% is real; it simply does not
need the certificate. That is worth publishing as a benchmark result, because the alternative — quoting
94.7% as a CRI result — would attribute a descent algorithm's work to a duality argument.

The question that survives this study is therefore not "can CRI classify material revisions?" (it
cannot, and the active-set test already implies the same set) but **"when is a validated bound actually
the right object?"** Two directions look worth the next study, in this order:

1. **An identified economic mapping on an untouched sample.** The largest open weakness is that `c = -z`
is declared, not estimated: it is a modelling convenience whose scale sets both the materiality rate and
the certification rate. A study whose cost vector comes from an operational loss — a portfolio with a
real benchmark and transaction costs, a staffing plan with published service constraints — would make
"revisions move decisions" an economic claim instead of a statement about this quadratic. Without that,
the honest title of this paper is "revisions move *this* decision", and the next reviewer will say so.
2. **The regime where re-solving is not available.** CRI's only possible advantage is that it certifies
without solving. At four assets and three assets that regime does not exist, and the measurements here
say so. The test that could still favour it needs programs where a re-solve is genuinely expensive or
unavailable — large-scale or distributed allocation, a solver the auditor does not trust, or a decision
that must be audited years later by a third party with the record and no model. The stored-state result
above is the relevant caution: even there, the record must carry the full decision state.

## 6. Threats to validity

* **Decisions are counterfactual.** No historical operational decision is audited.
* **The mapping is declared, not identified.** `c = -z` with train-split standardisation; §4.8 varies the
  risk weight, not the functional form.
* **Sample size.** 38 test events and 10 clusters in cohort A; the interval says so. The holdout for A has
  four events.
* **Reanalysis, not confirmation.** The 2015–2024H1 sample had been inspected before these corrections, so
  its corrected results are a reanalysis. Every table and the abstract carry that label. The
  2024H2–2025H1 sample is untouched but tiny in A (4 events).
* **Unexecuted sensitivity families.** The prespecified mapping variants and the horizon grid were not
  run (§4.8, amendment A10). No claim in this paper is evidence about a different functional form or a
  different audit horizon, and the redundancy and geometry results in particular have not been shown to
  survive either change.
* **The baseline comparison is at four and three assets.** OSQP's advantage is measured on tiny convex
  programs; the result says nothing about dimensions where a re-solve costs seconds.
* **Dependence.** Events within a cohort share release calendars and macro shocks; the block bootstrap over
  years is a partial correction.
* **Fixed horizon.** Twelve calendar months is one choice; revisions continue afterwards, and at this
  horizon every input is revised in every event.
* **Vendor dependence.** ALFRED's archive is the measurement instrument, and a series' vintage history can
  itself be reconstructed by the vendor.
* **Category labels** are calendar heuristics, not agency-published classifications.
* **Small dimension.** Four and three assets; the KKT test's behaviour in higher dimensions is not measured
  here.
