# Do authentic data revisions change the decision? A real-time vintage study of certified revision impact

**Draft research paper.** Branch `research/real-world-revision-study`, from CRI commit `8e666a0`.
Every number below is generated by `scripts/research/rtds_build.py`, `scripts/research/rtds_study.py`
and `scripts/research/rtds_tables.py` from the archive in `artifacts/research/rtds/`. The protocol was
written and committed before the confirmatory run (`PREREGISTRATION.md`).

## Abstract

Macroeconomic observations are revised, and much is known about how large those revisions are. Much
less is known about whether they change *decisions*. We assemble 2,347 real-time vintage responses
(675 distinct release dates) over seven US series from ALFRED — 392 decision/audit pairs spanning
2000–2024 — and run one fixed convex
resource-allocation program on them, measuring the decision regret of each revision by re-solving with
two independent solvers. In the confirmatory test split, **63.2%** (95% clustered interval 33.3–91.7%)
of quarterly BEA expenditure revisions and **47.4%** (44.4–55.6%) of monthly BLS payroll revisions
change the optimal decision by more than 1e-3 objective units. A certificate computed from the
original decision and its multipliers proves immateriality for 28.9% and 42.1% of events respectively,
covering 78.6% and 80.0% of the revisions that were in fact immaterial. It never once certified a
material revision as immaterial. Two negative results are as important as the positive ones: (i) the
certified set is **exactly** the set on which the classical active-set/KKT stability test already
holds, so on this data the certificate adds no classification power over a one-line post-optimality
test; and (ii) ranking revisions by numerical size is uninformative about decision materiality
(AUC 0.49 and 0.61), while decision geometry — the projection of the revision on the recorded
decision, active-set configuration and distance to a transition — predicts it well (AUC 0.88 and 0.94).

## 1. Question, and what is not claimed

The question is: *of the authentic revisions examined, how many materially change the decision, and how
accurately can that be established without re-solving?*

The economic observations are real vintage data. **The decisions are not historical.** They are
generated by a convex program this study defines, so the design is a counterfactual decision-model
study, not an audit of decisions anyone actually took. No claim is made that the growth-to-cost mapping
is empirically identified: it is a prespecified linear transformation, and §4.6 reports how much the
conclusions move when it is changed. Nothing here is a new theorem; the certificate is weak duality
plus an active-set argument, applied to real revisions.

## 2. Data

Source: **ALFRED**, the archival companion of FRED (Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis), read through its
public real-time endpoints. Two cohorts, analysed separately and never pooled, because they are
different revision processes:

| cohort | frequency | series | vintages | decision events (train / pilot / test) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | quarterly | `GDPC1`, `GPDIC1`, `GCEC1`, `EXPGSC1` | 1,338 | 52 / 8 / 38 |
| B | monthly | `PAYEMS`, `MANEMP`, `USCONS` | 1,009 | 156 / 24 / 114 |

Each vintage is one release as it stood on its release date; `alfredgraph.csv?id=…&vintage_date=…`
returns the *vintage* series, not the current one, which we verified before writing the protocol
(GDPC1 for 2014-10-01 reads 16,311.6 at 2015-01-30, 16,294.7 at 2015-03-27 and 16,186.7 at 2016-07-29).

**Revision categories are kept apart**: advance/second/later estimates and the annual-benchmark window
are labelled from the release calendar, and *no revision is described as an error*. A revised
observation is a new estimate of a quantity, not a correction of a mistake; the BEA and BLS revisions
in this sample are overwhelmingly the ordinary re-estimation process.

**Time policy.** A vintage date is a release date, not an intraday timestamp. Every vintage is treated
as available only at 23:59:59 UTC on its date, and a decision clock is the first date whose release is
complete. This can only delay eligibility, never advance it, so it cannot create look-ahead. Intraday
availability is not claimed anywhere.

**Accessibility finding.** The Philadelphia Fed's Real-Time Data Set for Macroeconomists was assessed
first and not used: its documented data-file paths (`/-/media/.../real-time-data/`) now return the
site's 404 page, so a study built on it would depend on an undocumented URL. ALFRED's endpoints are
stable, dated to the day, and complete for these series. `PCEC1` (real personal consumption) has no
ALFRED vintage archive (its download page 404s), which is why the quarterly cohort uses the four real
expenditure aggregates that do.

**Rights.** ALFRED/FRED data are redistributed by the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis under its terms
of use; the underlying series are US government statistics in the public domain. This repository stores
derived event records, URLs and SHA-256 digests, not the vendor's raw files (a git-ignored cache).

## 3. Protocol

* **Splits** (frozen): train 2000–2012 for the covariance, standardisation and classifier; pilot
  2013–2014 for pipeline validation only, excluded from confirmatory estimates; test 2015-01 – 2024-06.
* **Program**, identical in structure across cohorts:
  `min 0.5 x'(ρ·corr(G))x − c'x` s.t. `Σx = 1`, `0 ≤ x ≤ 0.6`, plus `x_gov ≥ 0.15`, `x_inv ≤ 0.45`
  (A) or `x_manuf ≥ 0.20` (B); `c` is the standardised growth vector, `G` the train-split first-print
  growth matrix.
* **Event**: one observation period, the vintage that first printed it, and the vintage four quarters
  (A) or twelve months (B) later. Multiple revised inputs on one release are one decision event.
* **Truth**: regret `f_A(x_D) − f_A*` with `f_A*` from the internal solver and OSQP; the event is
  material when `regret > τ = 1e-3`.
* **Statistics**: moving-block bootstrap over decision years (the clustering unit), 2,000 resamples;
  one primary test per cohort; everything else secondary.

**Amendment (recorded before the confirmatory run).** The protocol fixed `ρ = 0.05` without knowing
its scale relative to standardised growth. A prespecified rule on the **train split only** chose ρ from
{0.25, 0.5, 1, 2} to maximise the share of interior optima; all four values tied at 1.0, so the
tie-break selected ρ = 1.0. The protocol's original values (0.02, 0.05, 0.2) remain in the sensitivity
grid reported in §4.6. The rule turned out to be non-discriminating — with `Σx = 1` a solution almost
never sits entirely on box bounds — so the choice of ρ is defended by the sensitivity grid, not by the
rule.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 H1: revisions do change decisions, often

| cohort | test events | material at τ=1e-3 | 95% cluster interval |
|---|---|---|---|
| A (quarterly BEA) | 38 | 24 (63.2%) | [33.3%, 91.7%] |
| B (monthly BLS) | 114 | 54 (47.4%) | [44.4%, 55.6%] |

Both lower bounds exceed the protocol's 10% threshold, so H1 holds in both cohorts. The quarterly
interval is wide because there are only ten decision-year clusters; that width is the honest statement
about how much 38 events can support.

### 4.2 H2: immaterial revisions are certifiable

| cohort | certified immaterial | 95% interval | immaterial events with ‖Δc‖>0 | certified among them |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 28.9% | [8.3%, 58.3%] | 14 | 11 (78.6%) |
| B | 42.1% | [36.1%, 52.8%] | 60 | 48 (80.0%) |

Every revision in the test split has ‖Δc‖ > 0: a revision that leaves the cost vector exactly
unchanged is not a revision. So these are nonzero numerical changes whose decision impact is provably
below the threshold. **No material revision was ever certified immaterial** in either cohort — the
error that matters for a certificate — while 21.4% and 20.0% of immaterial revisions were not
certified (a power failure, not a soundness failure).

### 4.3 The certificate's certified set is the classical active-set set

| cohort | certified and KKT-stable | certified but not stable | stable but not certified |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 11 | 0 | 0 |
| B | 48 | 0 | 0 |

This is the most consequential result for the CRI programme. On authentic revisions, CRI's
residual/duality certificate certifies an event exactly when the recorded weights remain KKT-optimal
for the revised program — which is what a post-optimality active-set test already tells you, in one
least-squares solve and one feasibility check, without multipliers, a bound, or a certificate object.
CRI is not *wrong*; it is **redundant as a classifier**. Its extra content is quantitative: a bound
that is valid even when the decision is not stable. §4.4 shows that bound did not separate the
remaining events either.

### 4.4 H3: size does not predict materiality, geometry does

| cohort | AUC ranking by ‖Δc‖ | magnitude model | + revision metadata | + decision geometry |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 0.491 | 0.509 | 0.378 | **0.878** [0.762, 1.000] |
| B | 0.612 | 0.388 | 0.582 | **0.937** [0.891, 0.983] |

Ranking revisions by numerical size is at or near chance. Adding revision metadata — how many inputs
moved, the release index, a benchmark-window flag — does not fix it: in cohort B it recovers some
signal (0.58) but stays far below geometry, and in cohort A the fitted logistic model performs worse
than chance out of sample, which is what a 52-event training set buys. Decision geometry — the
revision projected on the recorded decision, the number of binding coordinates, the curvature
condition number, and the distance to an active-set transition measured by the stability radius —
predicts materiality well in both cohorts, with a clustered interval that excludes 0.75 in both.

The mechanism is not mysterious and is not a tautology: a large revision along coordinates the decision
does not hold is harmless, and a small revision that crosses an active-set boundary is not. What the
result establishes is that the *available* metadata (revision size) is the wrong sufficient statistic —
not that a deeper structural model would fail.

One caveat on the metadata set is structural, not statistical: at a four-quarter (twelve-month)
horizon **every** input in every event has been revised (median 4 of 4 for cohort A, 3 of 3 for B), so
"number of changing inputs" has no variance to explain. The metadata model is therefore tested on the
release index and the benchmark flag, and the negative result is about those, not about input counts in
general. A shorter horizon would give the count variance — and is the obvious next experiment.

### 4.5 H4/H5: where certification is useful, and what it costs

| cohort | certificate | OSQP re-solve | warm start | internal re-solve | audit record | full problem |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 1.77 ms | 0.86 ms | 3.64 ms | 3.68 ms | 429 B | 1,080 B |
| B | 1.49 ms | 0.85 ms | 3.60 ms | 3.76 ms | 366 B | 807 B |

* **Against OSQP, certification loses on time** (1.8–2.1× slower), confirming the synthetic finding.
  It beats the project's own SLSQP re-solve (≈2.4×) but that is not the competitor that matters.
* **Against warm-started reoptimisation** the certificate is competitive in wall-clock (1.5–2.4×
  faster) but warm start is a solver feature, not an audit artefact.
* **The genuine advantage is what the audit must retain**: 366–429 bytes per decision (weights,
  multipliers, digests) against 807–1,080 bytes for the full problem, and — more importantly —
  the certificate needs no problem reconstruction, no covariance matrix and no cost vector. That
  matters for a workflow that must answer *many* "has this decision become invalid?" queries against a
  stored decision without holding the model. It does **not** matter for a workflow that already holds
  the model and re-solves, where OSQP wins.
* **CRI-U is not useful at these revision scales.** The worst-case bound over a set sized by train-split
  revision dispersion never certifies anything (0 of 38 and 0 of 114), because the worst case over the
  declared set is dominated by corrections far larger than the ones observed. Reporting it as a
  competitor that loses is more informative than omitting it.

### 4.6 Sensitivity

| cohort | ρ | material τ=1e-2 | τ=1e-3 | τ=1e-4 | certified τ=1e-3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 0.02 | 44.7% | 57.9% | 57.9% | 42.1% |
| A | 0.2 | 44.7% | 55.3% | 57.9% | 42.1% |
| A | 1.0 | 42.1% | 63.2% | 63.2% | 28.9% |
| B | 0.02 | 23.7% | 28.1% | 28.1% | 71.9% |
| B | 0.2 | 28.1% | 29.8% | 31.6% | 65.8% |
| B | 1.0 | 30.7% | 47.4% | 54.4% | 42.1% |

Materiality and certifiability both depend on the risk weight: with a nearly linear objective (ρ small)
the decision sits on a corner and revisions rarely move it, so certification rises to 72% for B. The
*qualitative* conclusions (revisions are often material; magnitude is uninformative; geometry predicts;
certified set = stable set) hold across the grid. The precise percentages do not.

### 4.7 Revision category

Materiality is somewhat higher in the annual-benchmark window for cohort A (7 of 10 vs 17 of 28) and
lower for cohort B (3 of 10 vs 51 of 104). With ten events per benchmark cell these are descriptions,
not tests; no confirmatory claim is attached.

## 5. What this establishes, and what it does not

1. **Authentic revisions change decisions often enough to matter** — 47–63% of events in two
   independent revision processes, under a fixed, prespecified program.
2. **A certificate from the original decision is sound but weak here**: it never certifies a material
   revision as immaterial, and it covers four fifths of the immaterial ones, but it selects exactly the
   events a classical stability test selects.
3. **The interesting predictor is geometry, not magnitude.** This is a usable finding for practice: an
   operations-research team tracking published data cannot triage revisions by size.
4. **It does not establish that CRI is worth its machinery** in a workflow that can re-solve. The
   honest summary is that the certificate is a *soundness guarantee with no classification advantage
   over a one-line optimality test on this data*, and a *storage/audit-trail advantage* where the model
   is not retained.
5. It does not generalise beyond: one program family, one mapping, two cohorts, four and three
   dimensions, a fixed 4-quarter/12-month audit horizon, and the specific revisions ALFRED preserves.

## 6. Threats to validity

* **Decisions are counterfactual.** No historical operational decision is audited. The study measures
  the sensitivity of *a* decision rule, not the regret of a real firm.
* **The mapping is declared, not identified.** `c = z(growth)` with train-split standardisation is a
  modelling choice; §4.6 varies the risk weight but not the functional form.
* **Small samples.** 38 test events in cohort A with 10 clusters; the intervals say so.
* **Dependence.** Monthly events within a year share release calendars and macro shocks; the block
  bootstrap over years is a partial correction, and events within a cohort are not independent draws.
* **Horizon.** Four quarters / twelve months is one choice; revisions continue afterwards (the
  benchmark revision is often larger than the ones inside the window), and at that horizon every input
  is revised in every event, which removes variation from the "number of changing inputs" predictor.
* **Every event has a revision by construction.** Events where the panel was incomplete were dropped
  (none were, in either cohort), so the materiality rate is conditional on at least one input having
  changed — which is the question asked, but not the same as "share of releases that matter".
* **Vintage granularity.** Release-date precision only; intraday availability is unverified.
* **Vendor dependence.** ALFRED's archive is the measurement instrument; a series' vintage history can
  itself be revised by the vendor's reconstruction.
* **Category labels** are calendar heuristics, not agency-published revision classifications.

## 7. Reproduction

```
.venv/bin/python scripts/research/rtds_build.py     # fetch vintages (cached, hashed) and build events
.venv/bin/python scripts/research/rtds_study.py     # decisions, certificates, baselines, statistics
.venv/bin/python scripts/research/rtds_tables.py    # regenerate every table in this paper
```

The build is resumable: every vintage response is cached by series and date, and `dataset.json`
records the SHA-256 of each response together with a manifest digest, so a re-run can prove it used
byte-identical inputs.

Artifacts: `dataset.json` (events with per-input values and digests), `dataset.md`,
`results.json` (per-event rows, methods, statistics), `tables.md`. Raw vintages are cached under
`data/rtds_cache/` and are not committed; the manifest digest in `dataset.json` pins them.
